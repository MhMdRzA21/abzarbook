PDF too large to email?

Why it happens, and the fastest way out

The bounce from Gmail ("this message exceeds the 25MB limit") is almost never caused by the words on the page — text is cheap to store. It's the images: camera scans, slides exported with full-resolution photos, or a merge of several already-large files stacked into one.

Why is my PDF so large?

  • Scanned pages. A phone photo at 12 megapixels, times 10 or 20 pages, adds up fast — far more data than the same pages would need as typed text.
  • Merged packs. Merging several PDFs adds their sizes together; it doesn't compress anything in the process.
  • Embedded fonts and media in exports from PowerPoint, Keynote or InDesign, where every slide can carry its own full-size background image.
  • Redundant copies. A PDF that's been re-saved or re-exported several times can end up carrying old, unused image data nobody ever cleaned up.

What to try, in order

  1. Compress losslessly first. This recompresses the file's internal images and streams without re-rendering pages — text stays sharp and selectable. For many documents this is already enough.
  2. Still over the limit? Pick a size. Compress to a set size lowers image resolution in controlled steps until the file fits a limit you choose — 25MB for Gmail, or less for a stricter portal. This does reduce image sharpness, by design, which is why it's a second step rather than the default.
  3. Split and send only the range the other person needs.
  4. Rescan at a lower resolution — only realistic if you still have the paper original. 200–300 DPI is plenty for a document that just needs to be readable.

No compressor can force every scan under a tiny limit without visibly degrading it — a 40-page color scan is not going to become 2MB and stay sharp. If lossless compression isn't enough and the target-size tool still can't hit your limit cleanly, splitting the file is the honest next step, not a smaller and smaller quality setting.
